Installation guide: balancing robot V1.1

From a box of parts to a robot that balances, stands up and takes voice orders through Claude. Take it in order: each stage checks the one before it. Allow a weekend for the build, an evening for the software.

Everything referred to here is in robot_software_V1.1.zip and V1.1_print_files.zip. Part numbers, prices and links: the BOM ("BOM" sheet). Every wire: the BOM's "Wiring" sheet.

0. What you need on your computer

  • PlatformIO (VS Code extension or pip install platformio) to build and flash the ESP32-S3.
  • Raspberry Pi Imager to write the Pi's SD card.
  • LKMTECH motor tool (Windows) + a USB-CAN adapter (Neo2) to set up the motors once.
  • Optional, for the simulation: Python 3.11+, pip install mujoco numpy scipy, and g++.
  • A Claude API key (console.anthropic.com → API keys). Voice orders need it; everything else works without it.

2. Configure the motors (before they go in)

With the LKMTECH tool and the USB-CAN adapter, one motor at a time:

  1. Set ID 1 = left, ID 2 = right, CAN 1 Mbps.
  2. Set a current limit of 16.5 A.
  3. Send +0.2 N·m to each with the wheel free. Note the direction. Both must turn so the robot would roll forward once mounted; if one doesn't, flip DIR_LEFT / DIR_RIGHT in firmware/esp32/include/config.h later.
  4. Read the encoder: it must span 0–65535 per turn (ENC_COUNTS in config.h).

3. Assemble

Follow the BOM's "Build check" sheet. Short version:

  1. Motors onto the centre wall (8 × M2.5×10, blue threadlocker), connector bay pointing up. Plug the 6-pin and 3-pin leads into the bay from above, then thread them through the window in the wall into the centre gap.
  2. Wheel adapters + wheels. Measure the wheel offset first; if it differs, set NECK_L in cad_scripts/wheel_adapter.py and reprint.
  3. Floor tray: 5 V regulator, distribution board + 30 A fuse under the clamp bar, IMU on the axle line.
  4. Left wall: IP2368 USB-C charger on its carrier plate. Shelf: lidar adapter board. 4a. Front panel extras (Nov 2026): - AI Camera: first set its focus to ~1.5 m with the supplied tool (look at a printed page at 1.5 m on the live view), then 4 × M2×6 onto the 8.5 mm posts. Glue the 19 × 19 mm polycarbonate window into the pocket on the outside (2 dots of clear glue in the corners, not over the lens). - Headlights: solder ~12 cm of red/black 24 AWG to each COB disc (+ outer, − inner pad), drop the disc into its collar from the inside (LEDs facing out), thread the wires through the retainer's hole, 2 × M2×6. - Headlight driver (10 × 8 holes cut from the 2 × 8 cm perfboard of the set): Pololu D36V6F12 (VIN, GND, VOUT), IRLB8721 laid flat (gate via 100 Ω from the PWM pad, 100 kΩ gate to GND, source to GND, drain = the discs' −), VOUT -> both discs' +. Pads at the corners: VIN/GND bottom-inner, PWM/GND bottom-outer, discs at the top. Drill Ø2.2 in the two bottom corners (19 mm apart), 2 × M2×6 + washers onto the bosses left of the sonar. - Feed: PDB + -> 1 A inline fuse -> VIN, PDB − -> GND (22 AWG, up the shelf cable hole). Pi pin 32 (GPIO12) -> PWM, pin 34 -> its GND (Dupont). - Bumpers: 4 rubber bumpers on the face, Y ±66 mm, Z 40 and 127 (about 23 mm in from each side edge).
  5. Audio (new in V1.1): - Speaker into the speaker ring, ring onto the right wall over the grille (3 screws). The MAX98357A amp goes on the ring's tab with zip ties. 470 µF across the amp's 5 V input. - The two ICS-43434 mic boards into the front panel pockets, port side to the Ø1.5 holes, held by the mic clamps. Left mic SEL → GND, right mic SEL → 3.3 V. Twist the I2S wires with GND, keep them short. - Charge diode + 5 A fuse: inline in the dock-pad lead (heat-shrink), no plate on the right anymore.
  6. Slide the shelf in flat on the wall tops and screw it down: 4 × M3×10 countersunk into the wall-top inserts. These screws are structural (they tie the motor walls together; without them the walls splay under load). Then the battery with the BMS against its back face, both under the strap.
  7. Perfboard: build it from the to-scale layout on the wiring page ("Board pictures", perfboard drawn from the component side) on a standard 5 × 7 cm board with 24 × 18 holes: DevKit on two 22-pin female header rows (holes 2-23 counted from the right, rows 9 and 18 from the bottom), the 74AHCT125, 12 resistors and the dividers flat under the DevKit, plugs J1-J7 and the 470 µF below it. J1 is an XT30 PCB plug (the whole 5 V rail goes through it, too much for a 2.54 mm header). Then perfboard (CAN board on 2.5 mm spacers, 2 × M3 nylon through Ø3.2 holes you drill) and Pi (4 × M2 + washers) on the back cover; contact plate with the M4 contacts outside. The lidar's USB adapter plugs into the Pi's OTG port with a 90° micro-USB OTG cable (a straight adapter hits the wheel arch). Wiring routes and lengths: README, "Wiring in place", and the viewer's Wiring view.
  8. Front panel: camera, sonar, eyes + diffusers + retainers, mics. USB-C charging socket in the left side wall above the wheel (2 × M3 + nuts), so the robot can charge standing, face-down or on its back.
  9. Front panel and back cover: 6 × M3×10 countersunk hex-socket screws each (ISO 10642, 2.5 mm key), heads flush: nothing sticks out to mark the floor when the robot falls. Same for the 4 contact-plate screws. Lidar on top, power button (front-right) and arm button (front-left). Power switch: Pololu 2813 board screwed (or VHB-taped) inside the back cover, above the Pi (pads facing forward): VIN = BAT+ from the 30 A fuse, VOUT = BAT+ to the PDB (16 AWG); the two negatives (BMS P- and PDB in-) spliced beside it, a 26 AWG tap from the splice to its GND. The 12 mm button's two wires go to the board's pushbutton pads (in parallel with its own button); J9 (GPIO21) to its OFF pin. Before the first battery connection: VIN-VOUT open when off, no continuity between + and -. Cover plugs (so the back cover comes off as one module): the switch's input pair (VIN + P- splice) ends in a MALE XT60, the BMS lead in a FEMALE one; the switch's output pair (VOUT + splice) ends in a FEMALE XT60, the PDB lead in a MALE one (they can't be swapped; no bare live male). JST-XH 3-pin in the power-button lead beside the switch board, JST-XH 2-pin in the CAN pair beside the CAN board. Leave 4 cm of slack on every wire that goes to the cover. Motor leads: route them up out of the plug, over and down through the wall window in a loose loop (the shelf is 18 mm above the plug for this); don't pull them tight against the plug.
  10. Sense wires to J7: VBAT from the power switch VOUT, dock sense from the charge fuse+diode input, USB sense from the IP2368 VBUS pad (26 AWG each). 3 A inline fuse in the PDB -> regulator VIN lead.
  11. IR receivers: a TSOP38238 in each black hood, dome facing the window, leads bent straight down. Thread the leads through the Ø4.5 hole in the top skin, glue the hood on (window facing out: front-left, front-right, back-left, back-right). Pin order seen from the dome: OUT, GND, VS. Chain VS and GND from receiver to receiver, one OUT wire each; 6-wire cable to perfboard plug J8 (3V3, GND, GPIO13 FL, 14 FR, 15 BL, 16 BR).

4. Power-up checks (battery still disconnected where noted)

  1. CAN: about 60 Ω between CAN H and L, power off.
  2. 5 V rail on a bench supply first: 5.0–5.2 V at the Pi and the amp.
  3. Charging, before the battery is connected: IP2368 output 25.2 V; dock rods 25.2 V, + on the rod that meets the robot's + pad (the LEFT rod looking at the dock face); robot leaned back on the dock by hand: 25.2 V on the M5 nuts inside; pads at 0 V when undocked.
  4. First charge supervised, in a LiPo bag.

The dock

  1. Rods: brass Ø4 × 25 mm, solder a Ø5.5 brass washer on as a collar 16.5 mm from the tip and a 40 cm wire to the back end. Push the rod in from the back (tip out through the Ø4.3 bore), spring behind the collar, then epoxy an M3 washer over the back of the pocket (wire through its hole). It must slide freely, ~4 mm.
  2. Rod wires up the column to the controller board in the mast; the DC jack in the base's back edge is wired up to the board too (step 5).
  3. Head onto the foot (its groove over the foot's ring, a little glue, 2 × M3×16 down through the shelf behind the column into the foot's inserts), mast on (spigot into the column top, a drop of glue), TPU saddle inserts pushed up into the track frames from below, frames into their T slots, wings on (2 × M3×12 each into the slab-side inserts), 4 rubber feet; stand the dock against a wall with ~1 m of free floor in front. Keep the ridges clean and flat on the floor (no rug under them): they are what holds the robot on the dock.
  4. Reflective tape: cut two 141 mm strips of 25 mm retro-reflective tape and press them into the two recessed guides on the column face. This is what the lidar locks onto (it reads >200 intensity, walls 60–160).
  5. Dock controller (IR beacon + safe rod switch): build the 40 × 30 mm board from the site's Wiring diagram → Dock page (every connection and part value is there; schematic logic in firmware/dock_beacon/). Flash dock_beacon.ino onto the ATtiny85 (ATTinyCore, 8 MHz internal, ISP programmer or an Arduino as ISP). Push the 4 all-round TSAL6200 into the mast's side holes and 1 into the centre tube (a drop of glue), stand the board in the mast's hollow, run the jack and rod wires down the column (+ = the LEFT rod looking at the face). Never wire the DC jack straight to the rods: the board keeps them dead unless the robot is on them.
  6. Bench test the dock before the robot touches it (10 MΩ multimeter): - nothing on the rods: 0 V on them, status LED blips every 2 s; - 112 kΩ (100 k + 12 k) across the rods: 25.2 V after 0.5 s, LED on; remove it: 0 V within 2 s; - 1 Ω / 5 W across the rods: stays off, LED blinks; 3 shorts: fast blink until unplugged; - a phone camera sees the IR LEDs flicker.

First docking

  1. Check the beacon: the web app's telemetry ir value shows non-zero bits when a receiver hears it; turn the robot by hand and watch them move round. Then put the robot anywhere within ~4 m of the dock (any heading, also behind it) and press Dock & charge.
  2. It turns until it hears the beacon or sees the tape, drives towards the beacon (circling the column if it came from behind), lines up 45 cm out on the dock's axis facing away, reverses in steering on the two tape strips, leans back 8° onto the face and goes to CHARGING (blue LED). If the contacts don't touch it stands up, lines up again and retries (3 tries).
  3. That first dock saves the dock's position on the map (data/dock.json). From then on Dock & charge (or "go charge" by voice) works from anywhere on the map, and the robot docks by itself when the battery runs low ("auto_dock": false in config.json turns that off).
  4. Undock stands it up off the dock and drives it 0.4 m forward.

5. Flash the ESP32-S3

cd firmware/esp32
./sync_core.sh            # copies the simulation-tested controller from ../../core
pio run -t upload         # USB-C on the DevKitC
pio device monitor -b 115200

On boot, with the robot upright and still, the log must show acc ≈ (0, 0, +9.8). Tilt the top forward: ax goes negative and gy positive. If not, fix the IMU_MAP_* macros in config.h. Compare vbat with a multimeter and adjust VBAT_DIV.

6. Set up the Raspberry Pi

  1. Raspberry Pi Imager: Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm Lite 64-bit. In the settings, set hostname (e.g. robot), user pi, your Wi-Fi, and enable SSH.
  2. Copy the software: scp -r robot_V1.1 pi@robot.local:/home/pi/robot
  3. Install (UART, SPI, I2S sound card, camera, Python packages, Vosk + piper voice models, the service): ssh pi@robot.local cd ~/robot/pi && ./install.sh It downloads ~150 MB of models; give it 15–20 minutes on the Zero 2 W.
  4. Claude API key. It is kept out of config.json, in a file only root can read: sudo nano /etc/robot.env ANTHROPIC_API_KEY=sk-ant-... Check: sudo ls -l /etc/robot.env shows -rw------- root root.
  5. sudo reboot. The robot service starts on its own.
  6. Open http://robot.local:8080 on your phone.
  7. Teach it faces: in the app, type a name, pick 3–5 clear face photos of that person (front, slightly left/right, good light), press Teach face. It reports how many photos had a usable face. Then "Follow me" or say "robot, follow Anna".

Settings (pi/config.json)

  • lidar_offset_deg: with a wall straight ahead, set it so the wall reads at 0°.
  • ai_camera: true with the AI Camera (detections + follow); false for a plain Camera Module 3 (stream only).
  • auto_dock: true = go to the charger by itself when the battery is low (once it knows where the dock is).
  • voice.wake_phrase: words that start an order ("hey robot|robot").
  • voice.push_to_talk_only: true = only the app's Talk button listens.
  • voice.language: "de" for German, with vosk-model-small-de-0.15 and a German piper voice.
  • voice.stt: "vosk" (offline, on the Pi) or "http" with stt_url pointing to a Whisper server.
  • voice.mic_gain_db: raise if it misses you at 2 m, lower if it clips.

After changing it: sudo systemctl restart robot.

7. Test the audio

arecord -l                                   # the "googlevoicehat" card must be listed
arecord -D plughw:CARD=sndrpigooglevoi -c 2 -r 48000 -f S32_LE -d 5 /tmp/t.wav
aplay -D plughw:CARD=sndrpigooglevoi /tmp/t.wav
speaker-test -D plughw:CARD=sndrpigooglevoi -c 2 -t sine -f 440 -l 1

Talk from 2 m during the recording: you should hear yourself clearly, with both channels alive (sox /tmp/t.wav -n stat per channel if in doubt). A silent channel means a SEL pin or DOUT wire problem; noise only means BCLK/LRCL.

Then in the app: tap Talk, say "drive forward half a metre". The status line shows the recognised text, Claude's tool calls and the reply, which the robot speaks. Saying "Stop!" works instantly, without Claude.

8. First balance

  1. Wheels off the ground, press arm (button or app): the wheels should react to tilting the body.
  2. On a clear floor of at least 2 × 2 m, hold it upright and arm. It should balance on its own.
  3. Lay it on its face, then on its back: with auto stand-up on, it gets up by itself (it needs ~0.7 m of free floor).
  4. Joystick, turns, then Explore to build the lidar map. Tap the map to send it somewhere.

9. Run the tests and the simulation (optional)

cd pi && python3 -m pytest -q tests          # 20 tests, includes the voice pipeline with synthetic audio
cd sim && python3 build_model.py && python3 build_model.py --arena && python3 design_lqr.py 0.06 0.6 10
cd ../core && g++ -O2 -std=c++17 -shared -fPIC controller.cpp sim_api.cpp -o ../sim/libbot.so
cd ../sim && python3 run_tests.py 40

If you change the robot (heavier battery, other wheels), update sim/cad_mass.json or re-run cad_scripts/assemble.py, redesign the gains, then ./sync_core.sh and reflash.

Voice: talk to the robot, Claude gives the orders

Hardware, all on the Pi's I2S bus (dtoverlay=googlevoicehat-soundcard, same mic + amp pair as the Google AIY Voice Kit): - 2 x ICS-43434 MEMS mics in the front panel, 124 mm apart (Y ±62, Z 100), 65 dBA SNR, behind Ø1.5 ports. - MAX98357A 3.2 W class-D amp on a tab of the printed speaker ring. - 40 mm 4-ohm speaker on the RIGHT side wall above the wheel arch (centre X −2, Z 86), firing sideways through a grille (the top belongs to the lidar). The right carrier plate is gone; the charge diode + fuse sit inline in the lead. Wiring: BOM, "Wiring" sheet.

Flow (pi/robot/audio.py, speech.py, voice.py): 1. Mics at 48 kHz -> high-pass -> 16 kHz -> two-mic beam steered at the talker (GCC-PHAT) -> speech gate -> utterance. Speech from 2 m arrives ~25 dB above the mic noise; the beam adds ~3 dB against room noise and gives the direction. 2. Speech-to-text: offline Vosk on the Pi, or a Whisper server (e.g. faster-whisper on the Jetson) via stt_url. 3. "Hey robot, ..." (or the Talk button / text box in the phone app) -> Claude (claude-haiku-5-5) with tools: drive, turn, stop, go_to (lidar map), explore, flip, set_eyes, headlight, follow (a person, by name if taught), who_is_there (AI camera), look (camera photo), status. Limits enforced on the robot (0.6 m/s, 6 s per drive; obstacle avoidance and the sonar stop stay on). 4. Claude's answer is spoken (piper voice, or espeak-ng). The mics are muted while it talks. "Stop!" is handled locally, instantly, and interrupts whatever is running.

Setup: pi/install.sh (overlay, Vosk + piper models, /etc/robot.env); put ANTHROPIC_API_KEY=... in /etc/robot.env (root-only, loaded by robot.service). Settings: "voice" in pi/config.json. Not tested on hardware yet: the sound card, mic gain at 2 m in your room, piper speed on the Pi Zero 2 W, recognition with the motors running (tests use synthetic audio and a fake Claude client). The sim now also clips the IMU at its real range (±2000 dps, ±16 g).

Stress check (FEA, CalculiX)

fea/fea.py meshes the floor + motor-wall block (gmsh, tet10) and solves it with CalculiX: peak motor torque 2.3 N·m per motor plus 25 N per wheel (≈ 3 g), loads through the 4 motor screws, supports at the 4 floor bosses of the front panel / back cover screws. python3 fea/fea.py reinf (current design, shelf screwed), NOSHELF=1 to leave the shelf out, then python3 fea/fig2.py for the pictures.

  • The floor + motor walls are a separate piece from the shell (the wheel wells cut the floor): the front panel, back cover and the screwed battery shelf hold them together. Tighten all of them.
  • Before (shelf only sliding on dovetail rails): max ~20 MPa at the wall roots and front floor bosses, walls splay, motor axis tilts 3.3°. The wheel's 61 mm lever arm, not the torque, does this.
  • Now (shelf screwed to the wall tops with 4 × M3, 45° root fillets, wall-to-boss ribs): max 5.4 MPa, motor axis tilts 0.24°. (Computed with the cable slot open to the top; the final closed window only adds material.) PETG layer bond is ~20–30 MPa.

USB-C charging socket

In the left side wall above the wheel (X 8, Z 66), so the robot can be charged standing, lying face-down or on its back. A plugged-in USB-C cable clears the tyre by 8 mm; the IP2368 carrier plate on that wall now ends 20 mm below its screw line (z 72) to leave room for the socket.

Panel screws

Front panel, back cover and contact plate use countersunk hex-socket M3×10 (ISO 10642), 0.2 mm below the surface, so nothing sticks out to mark the floor when the robot falls. The panels are 1.5 mm thicker (4.5 mm) on their inside around each screw so 2.4 mm of plastic stays under the head; the body's bosses moved 1.5 mm inwards to make room. Stress check after the change: 5.3 MPa peak (was 5.4), motor-axis tilt 0.23°. Inserts: only M3 and M2 (the Pi and the IMU take M2 screws with a washer). Perfboard: standard 5 × 7 cm, 24 × 18 holes.

The three M3 screws holding the front and back panels to the body sit 2 mm further from the panel edges than before (top front (Y_IN-6, 75), top back (76.6, 119.6), floor (40, Z_FLOOR+5.5)), so there is more material around each hole.

Motor plugs, clearances

  • The MF6015's connectors (a 6-pin and a 3-pin header) sit in a 15 mm bay on top of its Ø49 × 8 mm rear boss; the plugs go in from above. Mount the motors with that bay pointing up, plug them in before the shelf, and thread the leads through the window in each wall (24 x 16 mm, rounded) into the centre gap; a 2 mm notch above the bay lets the 3-pin plug go straight down. (An open slot printed as a 20 mm overhang: the window prints as a 16 mm bridge.)
  • The shelf sits at z 37 (4 mm higher than before): 4 mm of air above the plug leads.
  • 3 mm of air all around each motor (front, back, floor; 8 mm to the shelf): the body is 67 mm deep (was 62).
  • Motor pockets are teardrops pointing to the back (the body prints on its front face): no flat overhang.

What the simulation taught us (and how the code handles it)

  • Stand-up = kick + energy shaping + catch.
  • The kick: full push towards the side the top points to.
  • Energy shaping: the motors then trim the body's energy so it arrives upright with almost no speed left.
  • The catch: LQR takes over below 20°.
  • The stand-up needs about 0.7 m of free floor, and the wheels briefly reach ~4 m/s.
  • It learns its own robot.
  • If a stand-up falls back, the next attempt aims higher. If it overshoots, it aims lower.
  • The firmware stores this correction in flash.
  • Low centre of mass means a big lean when accelerating.
  • The wheel torque leans the body much more than on a tall robot: 0.6 m/s² already gives about 9°.
  • Acceleration is therefore limited to 0.6 m/s² and yaw rate to 1.2 rad/s. In the simulation, wobble grows above about 1.8 rad/s; check this on the real robot.
  • The lidar tilts with the body.
  • The robot stands leaning about 4° (CoM 2 mm behind the axle). When braking it leans up to 17°.
  • The scan plane then hits the floor at 1–3 m. level_scan() removes those rays.
  • The map is only updated while the body is within 5° of level.
  • Scan matching corrects heading only.
  • Wheel odometry was good for distance. Letting the matcher also shift position made the map worse in tests.
  • The sonar sees what the lidar can't. An 18 cm post below the 21 cm scan plane is still avoided.
  • Gyro bias is learned whenever the robot is still (at boot, lying down, or charging).

Before the first power-up (bench checklist)

  1. Power switch: with a meter (battery out), VIN-VOUT open and no continuity between + and −. Battery in: one push of the top button switches on (board LED), another off. Boot log: encoder range detected: N counts/turn once the wheels have turned.

  2. Motors (LK PC tool + Neo2 adapter). - Set IDs 1 (left) and 2 (right), CAN 1 Mbps. - With the wheels off the ground, send +0.2 N·m to each motor. Both must turn so the robot would roll forward. If not, flip DIR_LEFT / DIR_RIGHT in firmware/esp32/include/config.h.

  3. Encoder range. Check that the 0xA1 reply's encoder spans 0–65535 per turn (ENC_COUNTS).
  4. IMU axes. - Upright and still: the boot log must show acc ≈ (0, 0, +9.8). - Tilt the top forward: ax goes negative and gy is positive while tilting. - If not, change the IMU_MAP_* macros.
  5. Battery divider. Compare the telemetry vbat with a multimeter, then adjust VBAT_DIV.
  6. CAN bus. About 60 Ω between CAN H and L with the power off. 5a. Motor-side CAN timeout (LK PC tool). Set each motor's communication-timeout / "CAN disconnect protection" so it stops when commands stop (≈50–100 ms). Then, wheels off the ground, motors spinning at +0.2 N·m, unplug the ESP32's USB or hold its reset: both wheels must stop within that time. If the tool has no such setting, a crashed ESP32 leaves the last torque applied: tell me and the firmware gets a hardware watchdog that cuts the XT60 path. 5b. CAN bus-off recovery. Robot armed, wheels off the ground: pull the CAN plug from motor R for ~1 s and reconnect. The log shows CAN bus-off #1: restarting the controller, then CAN back after … ms; the robot disarms (motor fault) and arms again with the button. 5c. TVS on the 6S rail. 5KP24A soldered across the PDB output (band to +). At full charge (25.2 V) it must stay cold.
  7. First balance tests. Hold the robot upright and press arm (button or app). Use the LiPo bag and a clear floor of at least 2 × 2 m.
  8. Lidar angle. With a wall straight ahead, set lidar_offset_deg in pi/config.json so the wall reads at 0°.
  9. IP2368. Measure 25.2 V at its battery output before connecting the pack.

Range for a good fix: ~1.2 m. - IR beacon (firmware/dock_beacon, ATtiny85, 38 kHz): code 'A' (600 µs bursts) from 4 LEDs all round the column top, code 'C' (1200 µs) from 1 LED in a tube on the face, a ±6° beam along the dock's axis. Robot: 4 TSOP38238 receivers in black hoods on the top skin at (±15, ±54) mm — symmetric, fully on the flat top (IR_RX_XY), 90° windows facing the four diagonals — on GPIO13–16; the ESP32 decodes them (ir_decode.h, 100 ms windows) into the telemetry ir byte. Which receivers hear 'A' gives the bearing to ±22°; it carries from metres away (typically 3–5 m, more in line of sight). - Estimate: every tape fix is a measurement of the same fixed dock pose in the odometry frame; they are averaged (close fixes weigh more) and odometry carries it between scans, so the steering doesn't jitter with lidar noise.

Dock electronics (firmware/dock_beacon/, schematic on the site: Wiring diagram → Dock): one ATtiny85 runs the IR beacon and a rod switch. Safety extra-low voltage only (25.2 V from a certified, short-proof charger), a 1.1 A PTC fuse, a TVS and a reverse-polarity MOSFET at the input. The rods are dead unless the robot is on them: a weak sense current (≤ 0.25 mA) looks for the robot's own 112 kΩ detect divider; only after 0.5 s of that does a high-side P-MOSFET put 25.2 V on them. A coin, key, hand, short or nothing keeps them off; a short while live switches off in ~10 ms (3 faults latch it off); every 2 s it drops the rods for 30 ms to check the robot is still there, so they go dead within 2 s of it leaving. Without firmware the switch is held off by its pull-ups. The logic is unit-tested (test_dock_logic.cpp, 16 checks) and modelled in the dock simulation.

How it docks (DockPilot, then ST_DOCK_LAND / ST_UNDOCK in the controller): 1. Known dock and far away: plan a path to its approach point first (the first dock saves its map pose in data/dock.json; low battery then docks by itself). 2. Find: turn on the spot until it sees the tape or hears the beacon. Home: turn to the beacon's bearing and drive to it (the closest lidar return near that bearing sharpens it); if it ends up behind or beside the column (something 45 cm ahead, no tape), it circles the column at 50 cm until the tape shows up. 3. Line up: back off if too close to turn, drive to the point 45 cm out on the dock's axis, turn to face away. 4. Reverse at 7 → 4 cm/s, steering onto the axis to ±1–3 mm (the robot can't slide sideways, so the lidar has to centre it, not the funnels). The distance is corrected for the body's lean (the lidar is 16 cm above the axle). 5. With the axle on the ridge's crest, the ESP32 leans it back under control (a pitch target): it rolls back down into place as it leans. Contacts touch → damping only → still → CHARGING, motors off. 6. Undock: wheels roll back under the body (speed-limited) until upright, then normal balancing; it drives 0.4 m out over the ridge. If the contacts open again within a minute of docking, the Pi docks again.

What the simulation changed on the way: magnets strong enough to hold it made it spin its wheels leaving a slippery floor; free wheels without anything in front let it roll out like a ladder → the ridge. A 4–6 mm wave, or one raised 2 mm, made it stall and then surge into the dock; leaning from behind the ridge meant rolling down it into the dock uncontrolled → 1.5 mm, floor level under the wheel, lean starts on the crest. Stopping hard after the undock peel made the wheels hop and spin → hand over with the speed it has. The attitude filter trusts the gyro while landing/undocking/docked (the plate bumps fool the accelerometer by up to 5°).

Electrical checks in simulation (Nov 2026)

What the wiring check and the CAN unit tests could not see: real voltages, currents and bus timing. Scripts: sim/power_sim.py, elec/can_ground_check.py, results in sim/results/power_report.json and elec/can_ground_report.json.

Battery sag at stand-up (robot sim with the battery as open-circuit voltage + series resistance; 16 stand-ups per case, 4 charge levels × 3 pack resistances 35 / 75 / 120 mΩ): - Stand-up works down to an empty pack (19.8 V) with a bad, 120 mΩ pack: 191/192 overall, 180 at the first try. The one failure (half charge, 75 mΩ) is the same ~1-in-30 miss the fixed-voltage tests show from the back, not a power limit: the empty/bad cases all got up. - Peak current from the battery 25–33 A, above 30 A for at most 10 ms: the 30 A blade fuse (rated to carry 135 % for minutes) and the BMS's 40 A trip are not reached. - Lowest bus voltage 16.8 V (empty + bad pack): the 5 V regulator (needs > ~6 V) and the motor drives (7.4–32 V) keep running. The firmware's low-battery filter ignores these few-ms dips.

Braking energy (regeneration): - Braking sends up to 6 A back into the battery, up to 1.7 J per stand-up or hard stop. With a healthy BMS the pack absorbs it: the rail rises at most 0.7 V (25.9 V with a full, worn pack). - If the BMS blocks charge current (a cell at its over-voltage limit, e.g. right after a full charge with an unbalanced cell), that energy has only the ~0.9 mF of capacitors on the rail: 0.18 J is enough to reach 32 V, the drives' limit, in about a millisecond. Too fast for firmware. - Fix, two layers: a 5KP24A TVS diode across the PDB output (conducts from ~27 V, holds the rail ≤ ~30 V at 6 A; ~€1), and the firmware switches the motors off (motor fault, event 6S over-voltage … V: motors off) when the rail reads > 26.8 V twice in a row. Motors off ends the braking current (back-EMF stays below the rail).

CAN bus timing (1 Mbit/s, 500 Hz control loop): - 138 µs per frame worst case (bit stuffing); 3 frames per cycle while balancing = 21 % bus load (28 % while off). 9 retransmissions would still fit in one cycle. - New in the firmware: bus-off recovery. A noise burst or a loose plug can make the ESP32's CAN controller shut itself off; before this fix it never restarted (robot falls, needs a reset). Now LkMotors::service() restarts it: ~1.4 ms + one cycle, well under the 20 ms CAN timeout, so a single bus-off doesn't even disarm. Logged as CAN bus-off #n / CAN back after … ms. Unit tests: 68 checks (bus-off → recovery → restart, a second bus-off, a stuck bus → motor fault, then recovery).

Ground offset: the CAN pair has no ground wire; the transceiver (ESP32 ground) and the motors (their V−) meet at the PDB. Worst case 60 mV between them (16.5 A in a motor's 6 cm 16 AWG return + 4.7 A in the perfboard's 14 cm 20 AWG ground); the SN65HVD230 tolerates −2…+7 V: 33× margin.

Still for the bench: the motors' own CAN timeout (5a), real reply delay, motor PWM noise into the IMU/mics/amp, a USB ground loop with the battery connected, and the BMS's real over-voltage threshold (read it in its datasheet: ≥ 4.25 V/cell wanted).
