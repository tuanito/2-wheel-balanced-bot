Balancing robot V1.1 — measuring a staircase by nodding

The robot has to know how tall the first step is before it can think about going down it. It has one lidar, 231 mm up, which sees nothing of the floor while it is upright — so it has to tip forward, and a two-wheel balancer cannot tip forward without driving forward. Ten episodes: what that costs, and every way it goes wrong.

How to read the clips. The yellow fan is the lidar, drawn where its rays actually land; the stairwell walls are made see-through for the camera only. Each clip ends with what that episode really did, taken from its own telemetry after it ran — not from what it was meant to show. A success means all three at once: the riser measured to within 20 mm, the robot still upright, and at least 100 mm of landing left between the wheels and the drop. The clips that work are selected episodes: the recipe succeeds on 37% of staircases, so the environment was asked for seeds until the outcome matched, and the seed is listed under each clip. The honest rate is the 0.37 above, not 5 of 10.

Ten episodes

3 clean successes, 2 right for the wrong reason, 5 failures.

Why it is this hard

Where the first tread is visible from, measured kinematically: body pitch in degrees that puts returns on it, against how far back the robot stands. Beyond about 550 mm there is no angle that works — the ray that clears the step nose already overflies the whole first tread.

At a 170 mm riser that band is one three-degree cell wide per standoff, which is why a fixed recipe scores 0 out of 10 there while managing 0.60 at 130 mm. And the nod is not free: gravity acting over the time the body is tilted adds about 1 m/s of forward speed per 34° of lean, and shedding it costs 0.2–1.1 m of travel towards the drop. Entering the nod reversing is what pays for it; entering it rolling forwards loses the robot outright, which is the clip above.

Three ways the reward was wrong

Each failure was the optimiser reading my numbers correctly.

Never look

At -2 for never looking against -10 for the drop, refusing to nod is correct arithmetic: a nod with an unlearned depth goes over the edge 30% of the time, so looking was worth -3 against a certain -2. The policy stopped nodding altogether - not one nod in twelve deterministic episodes.

Look, then fall downstairs

At -8 against -8, measuring and then going over was worth +12 +2 -8 = +6, better than anything safe. That policy measured the riser to 44 +- 48 mm against the script's 205 +- 286, four times more accurate, and drove off the edge in 30 of 30 held-out episodes.

Reverse off the back of the world

The simulated landing only ran 3 m behind the edge, and falling off it cost -5 while never looking cost -8. The policy drove backwards off the floor, which looked perfectly safe in the margin statistics. The landing now runs 8 m back.

The rule they share is now an assertion that runs when the environment is imported: the drop has to cost more than the best possible reading is worth, and never looking has to cost more than a safe bad look.
