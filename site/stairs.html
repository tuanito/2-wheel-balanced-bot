Balancing robot V1.1 — measuring a staircase by nodding

The robot has to know how tall the first step is before it can think about going down it. It has one lidar, 231 mm up, which sees nothing of the floor while it is upright — so it has to tip forward, and a two-wheel balancer cannot tip forward without driving forward. Twelve episodes of what that costs and every way it goes wrong, then ten staircases taken twice over — once with each way of looking, side by side.

What the pairs show that the averages do not. The swing's distinctive failure — the one the flick almost never has, 38% of episodes against 7% — is not a bad number, it is no number: it stops close, pitches down, and every ray clears the landing and lands on the tread. A riser is the difference between two surfaces, so hundreds of returns on the tread measure nothing with no landing left to subtract from. One of these ten clips fails exactly that way, with 597 rays on the tread and no number out of them. The flick does not have the problem because it takes its look from further back while reversing, so the landing stays in the scan; what it does instead, when it fails, is overfly the first tread and measure a surface two steps down.

Two ways to take the look

A flick is one-sided: gravity acts forward for the whole nod, so it costs 0.1–1.1 m of travel towards the drop. A swing leans back for as long as it leans forward, so it travels almost nothing — 650 mm of standoff became 638 mm across six cycles — and its amplitude grows each cycle, because the angle that sees a 170 mm riser is not the one that sees an 80 mm riser and the robot cannot know which it faces until it has measured it.

Same harness, same 200 held-out staircases, seeds 20000 onwards. The flick wins by 10 points, and at 2.1σ over 200 episodes that gap is real — an earlier 30-episode run put it at 14 points, which was 1.2σ and would have settled nothing. The swing loses twice over. It reads nothing at all in 38% of episodes against the flick's 7%, and it goes over the edge more often, 0.155 against 0.080, not less: it creeps to a fixed standoff and never reverses, so a misjudged stop keeps going forward, while backing away from the drop is part of what the flick is doing anyway. On a staircase whose riser is already known it is much the better look — 6 of 6 successes at 5 mm of error on a 100 mm riser, and it barely translates, 650 mm of standoff becoming 638 mm over six cycles. It loses across unknown staircases because the amplitude it can safely reach caps the angle, and a tall riser needs an angle it cannot get to from a standoff it can survive.

And it cannot be done from close in. Creeping to 150 mm is safe — the robot gets there without trouble and the swing is stable — and useless: 0 to 3 returns on the first tread, the riser read 469 mm wrong. Closer is worse, not better. The step nose subtends a larger angle the nearer you are, so the pitch needed to see past it rises: 41–50° at 200 mm, roughly 60–70° at 150 mm, while above about 0.8 rad of amplitude the swing loses the robot. The limit is the lidar sitting 231 mm up, not the controller. A cheap time-of-flight sensor at ~80 mm would see the first tread from 600 mm with a 20° nod and make the whole manoeuvre easy.

A sensor that does not need the nod

Every number above is limited by one thing. A ray reaches the first tread only if it clears the step nose and lands before the next one, which needs D < h × tread / rise, and the landing hides everything below it short of the edge — so the vertical riser face is never visible from behind the edge, at any angle. The limit is the sensor's height. At the lidar's 231 mm a 170 mm riser is out of reach beyond 408 mm; at the sonar's 134 mm, beyond 237 mm. Mounting a depth sensor low would be the worst place for it.

A VL53L5CX does not reach further — it obeys the same geometry — it reaches simultaneously. The lidar has one scan plane and has to pitch the whole robot to put that plane inside the band, which costs travel towards the drop and is why the nod recipes answer a third of the time. 45° of vertical field of view spans every angle in the band at once, so the robot stands still at 260 mm and the answer arrives at 15 Hz. Four steps in a row

6 of 6 flights took all four steps. The cycle is: roll at a measured speed, commit 20 mm before an edge the array has already located, hold attitude through the landing, settle, back off to 75% of the tread — which is both where the array measures best and far enough to approach the next edge deliberately — and repeat.

Committing is the whole trick. Reacting cannot work: rolling off a lip at walking pace produces no free fall at all, because the wheel stays on the corner the whole way down, so the accelerometer never sees anything to react to. Meanwhile the velocity loop watches its support vanish and drives the wheels to −4.9 m/s. The robot does not need to detect the edge though — it measured it. Holding attitude across the lip with the speed loop off takes a 100 mm step from 0% to 92%, and a 170 mm step from 0% to 75%.

And it stays square. A step taken skewed lands on one wheel, which is the one failure the attitude hold cannot save: at 10° off square the success rate collapses to 10–15% against 65–90% when square. The yaw controller holds the heading the robot approached on, and across four landings the drift stays under 0.3°. Worth knowing: the 8 × 8 array cannot measure that skew — at a 280 mm standoff 5° moves the edge distance by about 1 mm, well under the range noise — so squareness has to be held, not sensed.

Going down: 10 attempts at a single step

3 landed it. Mapping first, because it decides the shape of any descent: the occlusion condition for the k-th tread reduces to the same D < h × tread / rise with no k in it, so a staircase is self-similar and anything that sees the first tread can geometrically see them all. What limits the map is resolution — 8 rows resolve about 2 to 2.5 treads, the second to 25–30 mm. A one-step lookahead, not a flight.

And it cannot take the step. Judged on staying under 46°, one step at a time: 25% at a 40 mm lip, 8% at 80 mm, 0% from 100 mm up. The enemy is not the drop, it is the robot's own velocity loop: one axle means both wheels leave the ground together, and for ~150 ms the balance law servos against nothing — unloaded wheels run from 0.56 to 5.78 m/s in 50 ms and the reaction throws the body to 90–110°. The accelerometer sees the whole event (|a| falls to ~2 m/s² in free fall, spikes to ~176 on touchdown), so the controller now holds the balance law off while airborne and damps the wheels only. Compare the two 40 mm clips: with the catch the body peaks at 7°, without it at 94°. It is a real fix for a real bug, and it is still not a step primitive. That needs to be trained, the way the stand-up was.

Why it is this hard

Where the first tread is visible from, measured kinematically: body pitch in degrees that puts returns on it, against how far back the robot stands. Beyond about 550 mm there is no angle that works — the ray that clears the step nose already overflies the whole first tread.

At a 170 mm riser that band is one three-degree cell wide per standoff, which is why a fixed recipe scores 0 out of 10 there while managing 0.60 at 130 mm. And the nod is not free: gravity acting over the time the body is tilted adds about 1 m/s of forward speed per 34° of lean, and shedding it costs 0.2–1.1 m of travel towards the drop. Entering the nod reversing is what pays for it; entering it rolling forwards loses the robot outright, which is the clip above.

Three ways the reward was wrong

Each failure was the optimiser reading my numbers correctly.

Never look

At -2 for never looking against -10 for the drop, refusing to nod is correct arithmetic: a nod with an unlearned depth goes over the edge 30% of the time, so looking was worth -3 against a certain -2. The policy stopped nodding altogether - not one nod in twelve deterministic episodes.

Look, then fall downstairs

At -8 against -8, measuring and then going over was worth +12 +2 -8 = +6, better than anything safe. That policy measured the riser to 44 +- 48 mm against the script's 205 +- 286, four times more accurate, and drove off the edge in 30 of 30 held-out episodes.

Reverse off the back of the world

The simulated landing only ran 3 m behind the edge, and falling off it cost -5 while never looking cost -8. The policy drove backwards off the floor, which looked perfectly safe in the margin statistics. The landing now runs 8 m back.

The rule they share is now an assertion that runs when the environment is imported: the drop has to cost more than the best possible reading is worth, and never looking has to cost more than a safe bad look.
